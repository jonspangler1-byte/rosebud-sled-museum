About the collection

Four hundred sleds, fifty-five makers, and the stories behind them.

The Rosebud Sled Museum is a private collection of about 400 antique American sleds from 55 makers, four of them from Illinois.

Jonathan Spangler, a historian, genealogist and author, restores sleds, races in an annual vintage sled race, and researches the companies that built them. Watch more on his YouTube channel, @sleddymercury.

Featured makers

The companies that made them

Ten of the great American sled makers.

American Acme

Iceplane

The story begins with American Toy and Novelty Works, opened in York, Pennsylvania, in 1917 by German immigrant Casper Oermann to make cider mills, berry presses and boys’ sleds. In 1927 it merged with Acme Wagon Works of Emigsville to become American Acme Co., which a newspaper that year reported had sold more than 150,000 sleds. Its sleds wore speedy names such as Royal Plane, Sky Plane and Monoplane, until the company turned away from sleds in the 1960s.

Flexible Flyer

S.L. Allen & Co. · Philadelphia

Samuel Leeds Allen, a Philadelphia Quaker born in 1841, built S. L. Allen & Co. on farm and garden tools, then looked for a winter product to keep his skilled workers busy all year. His answer was a steerable sled with flexible runners, patented in 1889 and named the Flexible Flyer. Success came slowly, but by 1939 the company was celebrating its five-millionth sled. Allen died in 1918; his company was divided and sold in 1968.

Paris

Paris Mfg. Co. · South Paris, Maine

Henry Franklin Morton began making sleds in 1861 in a West Sumner, Maine, kitchen, a family business shared with his wife, Lucilla Forbes. The firm moved to Paris Hill and then to South Paris for the railroad, and by the early 1900s it employed hundreds, making everything from painted children’s sleds to toboggans. In September 1909 a Norway, Maine, newspaper reported that Paris had shipped a large consignment of sled materials for Lt. Peary’s North Pole expedition.

Wagner

Wagner Mfg. Co. · Cedar Falls, Iowa

Adam Wagner learned the trade as a hardware clerk in Cedar Falls, opened a one-room shop of his own in 1902, and incorporated it in 1904 as the Wagner Manufacturing Company. Beside barn-door track and door strips it built ornate steel sleds like the Winner, with steel hand sleds patented June 28, 1904, and Daisy coaster wagons. The latest sled advertisements found date from 1923; by then the firm was turning to elevator door hardware, and later garage doors.

Kalamazoo

Kalamazoo Sled Co. · Kalamazoo, Michigan

The Kalamazoo Sled Company grew out of the Page Manufacturing Company, a maker of wooden buggy parts, and was reorganized under its new name on February 14, 1894. By 1905 it had absorbed the Columbia Sled Company, employed more than 100 people, and was counted the largest maker of children’s sleds in the world, best known for its Champion line. The Gladding Corporation bought the company in 1968 and moved operations to Maine in 1972.

Pictured: Speed Demon, c. 1938.

Pratt

Pratt Mfg. Co. · Coldwater, Michigan

Pratt Manufacturing Company of Coldwater, Michigan, issued a sled catalog that the University of Michigan library dates, tentatively, to about 1909. In 1945 Max A. Holbrook of Coldwater filed a patent for a simpler sled steering mechanism, assigned to Pratt and granted in 1951. Its Red Rocket sleds carry the stamp “A Product of Pratt Mfg. Co., Coldwater, Michigan.”

Pictured: “Chieftain” branded Red Rocket, c. 1940–1952.

Sherwood

Sherwood Bros.

Sherwood Bros. Mfg. Co. was founded in Canastota, New York, in 1914 by John and William Sherwood, in the former Patten & Stafford wheel-rake plant on James Street. Its 1920 catalog called the firm “Manufacturers of Sleds and Coaster-Wagons,” with models such as the Speeder and the Auto Bob. In the early 1920s it was one of the companies held by Canastota’s Watson Products Corp.

08

Hunt, Helm & Ferris

Harvard, Illinois

The company grew from a hay carrier Henry L. Ferris patented in 1883 and first built in the basement of a Harvard hardware store, with partners Charles E. Hunt and Nathan B. Helm. Its five-pointed star marked a long line of farm equipment, and its CannonBall line made sleds, coaster wagons and roller skates, branded so boldly “that the paint still reads across a room.” In 1931 the firm took the name Starline.

Period advertisement, Cannon Ball coaster wagons. Hardware Age, Oct. 8, 1925. Internet Archive, public domain.

09

Standard Novelty Works

William Wills and P. F. Duncan established the Standard Novelty Works in Duncannon, Pennsylvania, in 1904, and its Lightning Guider sleds were made there until 1988. In 1920 the plant was credited with producing more children’s sleds than any other American factory, 1,600 to 1,800 a day. It also built children’s wagons, porch swings and furniture before closing in October 1990.

Period advertisement, Lightning Guider Bob Sled. Hardware Age, Jan. 20, 1917. Internet Archive, public domain.

10

Auto Coaster Wheel Co.

Buffalo Sled Company started around 1899 in Buffalo, New York (some sources say 1889), and was organized around 1905 by John J. Schneider and Henry J. Tiedt (one source says John Snyder). In 1909 it bought the Orient Novelty Company of North Tonawanda and moved into that firm’s three-story factory at the foot of Schenck Street, where it made sleds (including the Gliderole) and snow shovels, adding coaster wagons in 1912. A fire on April 16, 1920, destroyed the wheel department and storehouses; the company rebuilt and, in July 1920, filed incorporation papers in Albany renaming itself the Auto-Wheel Coaster Company, formerly the Buffalo Sled Company, because coaster wagons had become the principal product and the sled side had deteriorated. The Buffalo office closed after the filing, though the Buffalo Sled name still appeared on products into the early 1920s as back inventory. It also operated at times on Ellicott Creek in Buffalo in the old A. B. Williams plant, in Preston, Ontario, and through a branch in Terre Haute, Indiana. At its peak the firm employed about 160 people, advertised heavily in Popular Mechanics, and from 1915 organized Auto-Wheel Coaster Clubs across the United States to “develop leadership in boys.” Principal products included the Auto Wheel Coaster Wagon, Fleetwing Steering Sled, Coaster King, Fleetwing, and Fleetwing Racer, along with scooters, pedal bikes, sled strollers, bobsleds, and snow shovels, marketed as “Health Builders for Children.” Auto-Wheel filed for bankruptcy in July 1964, with union strikes and competition blamed; Wheel Industries bought the company in September 1964 to resume production, but the effort was not successful. The old plant, then being converted to a pallet factory, was destroyed by fire on Memorial Day, May 29, 1972 (some sources say May 31), a blaze that also took at least seven nearby homes.

Period advertisement, Auto-Wheel Coaster Co. Toys and Novelties, Aug. 1920. Internet Archive, public domain.

Timeline

Coasting Through History

Sledding in America. A timeline of sledding in America and the companies that made the sleds, prepared for the Gail Borden Public Library display in Elgin, 7–31 January 2027.

Americans were “coasting” downhill before the Revolution. Over the next two centuries, inventors, small-town factories and big national brands turned a homemade winter pastime into an industry, and Illinois played a part.

Before the Factories to 1858

  1. Long ago

    Indigenous peoples of the Subarctic and Northeast bend birch or larch boards into toboggans to haul game, goods and people.

  2. 1713

    Albany’s Common Council lets constables seize and break the “small & great slees” of children riding down the city’s hills (22 Dec). More bans follow in 1720 and 1726.

  3. c. 1760s

    Anne Grant later recalls Albany boys steering “little low sledge[s]” by rope down the main street, “perhaps a hundred at once.”

  4. 1775

    Boston schoolboys protest to a British general after ashes spoil the “coast” their fathers had used “for time immemorial.” Letters of 29–30 Jan are the OED’s first “coasting” in this sense.

  5. 1775–76

    Henry Knox’s “Noble Train” hauls the cannon of Fort Ticonderoga toward Boston on heavy wooden sleds.

  6. 1799

    Abijah Wilder patents “Runners and Bows for Sleighs and Sledges” (14 Dec), the earliest U.S. sled or sleigh patent found.

  7. 1813

    Salmon Hunt of Canaan, Conn., patents “steel plated sleigh shoes” (30 Dec), the earliest U.S. metal-runner patent found.

  8. 1836

    The Patent Office fire (15 Dec) destroys most early records. Wilder’s and Hunt’s patents survive only as index entries.

  9. 1837

    James Andrews of Camden, Maine, patents a “Holdback for Sleds” (U.S. 128), the oldest sled patent whose text and drawing survive.

  10. 1857

    Ads for “clipper sleds” appear by 1857. The name seems to be a trade type, not a patented design.

Patents & Painted Sleds 1859–1888

  1. 1859

    Benjamin P. Crandall of New York patents a “Child’s Sled,” a horse-head pull toy (U.S. 26,164). Massachusetts considers a law on coasting in public streets.

  2. 1861

    Henry F. Morton and Lucilla Forbes begin making sleds in Sumner, Maine. Their shop becomes Paris Mfg. Co.

  3. 1862

    J. N. Brown of New York patents a boys’ sled with a pivoted front runner steered by foot or cord (U.S. 34,874). It is the earliest steerable coasting-sled patent found.

  4. 1864 Illinois

    H. C. Hunt of Amboy, Illinois, patents a coasting sled steered by a rope on its front runners (U.S. 44,727), 25 years before the Flexible Flyer.

  5. 1871

    Adna Brown of Springfield, Vt., patents a three-wheeled coasting wagon that “may be used with sleds in winter” (U.S. 116,409). S. L. Allen & Co. incorporates in Philadelphia to make farm tools.

  6. 1877

    Charles R. Van Hoesen of New York patents a sit-up “Coasting-Sled” (U.S. 191,496). Henry F. Morton of Paris, Maine, patents a curved-frame boys’ sled (U.S. 193,380).

  7. 1879 Date uncertain

    E. B. Garton opens a woodworking shop in Sheboygan, Wis. (One collector’s book says 1889.) Toboggan clubs begin, starting in Montreal.

  8. 1882

    Pratt & Chase is organized in Coldwater, Mich., making cutters and children’s sleds. (A book’s 1870s start is unconfirmed.)

  9. 1883 Illinois

    Hunt, Helm, Ferris & Co. is founded in Harvard, Illinois, on a hay-carrier patent. Its sleds come later.

  10. 1886

    Orlando A. Thayer of Paris, Maine, patents a rope-worked steering attachment (U.S. 344,146). It is the likely source of the “Patent Steering Attachment” Paris sold in 1890.

  11. 1887–88

    Samuel L. Allen patents steerable double-runner bobsleds, including the costly “Fairy Coaster” type.

The Steering Age 1889–1929

  1. 1889

    Allen patents the Flexible Flyer (U.S. 408,681, 13 Aug). A crossbar flexes its spring-steel runners to steer.

  2. 1890

    The Paris catalog sells clippers, cutters and a 75¢ steering attachment. In Binghamton, N.Y., the Wilkinson Mfg. Co. takes its name.

  3. 1894

    The Kalamazoo Sled Co. is organized (14 Feb). By 1905 it calls itself the world’s largest maker of children’s sleds.

  4. 1904

    Standard Novelty Works of Duncannon, Pa., begins the Lightning Guider. Wagner Mfg. Co. of Cedar Falls, Iowa, patents a steel hand sled.

  5. 1905–06 Illinois

    Chicago’s parks put up about ten wooden toboggan slides, with an estimated 100,000 children riding them in a season.

  6. 1908 IllinoisDate uncertain

    Richards & Wilcox of Aurora, Ill., sells the No. 600 Little Giant folding sled. The 22 Dec 1908 patent date comes from a collector’s book and is not independently confirmed.

  7. 1909–10 Illinois

    Turner & Thomas of Chicago is selling its stamped-steel Steel Flyer by Dec 1909. S. J. Turner’s patent was filed 14 May 1909 and granted 13 Dec 1910 (U.S. 978,515).

  8. 1912

    Paris introduces its first steering sled, the Speedway (also spelled Speedaway), and switches to metal runners, according to collector’s books.

  9. 1914–16

    Sherwood Bros. of Canastota, N.Y., brings out the Auto Bob (1914). About 120,000 Flexible Flyers sell in 1915. Garton’s first steering sleds arrive in 1916, per a collector’s book.

  10. 1923–30s Illinois

    A toboggan slide stands at Swallow Cliff (Palos) by 1923. In the 1930s, CCC crews build more Cook County forest-preserve chutes.

  11. 1926 Illinois

    Howard J. Ferris of Hunt, Helm, Ferris & Co. (Harvard, Ill.) patents the Skeeboggan, a stand-up steel board (U.S. 1,578,415).

  12. c. 1929? Date uncertain

    Flexible Flyer’s “Sled of the Nation” poster appears. The artist’s signature and the ’29 date are not confirmed.

Speed, Saucers & Survivors 1930–today

  1. c. 1930–31 Date uncertain

    Flexible Flyer advertises a new “super-steering” Racer (Playthings, Feb 1931). A boys’ ad of about 1930 ties it to Admiral Byrd’s South Pole expedition.

  2. 1932

    At the Lake Placid Winter Olympics, the U.S. wins gold and silver in both bobsled events on the new Mt. Van Hoevenberg run.

  3. 1935–45

    Pratt Mfg. Co. makes 80,000 sleds for Christmas 1935. In World War II it makes only cots, and an iron-short Kalamazoo sells all-wood sleds.

  4. 1952

    Pratt Mfg. Co. of Coldwater closes, and its assets are auctioned on 25 Nov.

  5. 1956

    Kalamazoo’s Champion Flying Disc, an early plastic-and-fiberglass saucer, sells for about $6.

  6. 1965–67 Illinois

    Sherman Poppen invents the Snurfer, a snowboard ancestor, in Muskegon, Mich. (1965). Radio Steel of Chicago, today’s Radio Flyer, patents a Ski Sled (1966–67).

  7. 1968–70

    Leisure Group of Los Angeles buys S. L. Allen and makes Flexible Flyers in Medina, Ohio. Gladding buys Kalamazoo Sled Co. (1968) and Paris Mfg. Co. (1970). A Garton chronology’s “195?” Kalamazoo date is unresolved.

  8. 1973

    Investors buy Leisure Group’s toy division. As Blazon-Flexible Flyer, it makes the sleds in West Point, Mississippi. The Garton family sells its company.

  9. 1989–90

    Paris Mfg. Co. closes (1989), and its plant burns in Feb 1990. Standard Novelty closes in Oct 1990.

  10. 1993 Illinois

    Roadmaster buys the rights and makes Flexible Flyers in Olney, Illinois. Production moves to China in 1998.

  11. 2005–08 Illinois

    The Morton family’s Paricon buys the Flexible Flyer name (2005). Cook County approves tearing down its last big toboggan slides (2008).

  12. 2027 Illinois

    Coasting Through History: sleds from the Rosebud Sled Museum at Gail Borden Public Library, Elgin, 7–31 Jan.

Sources: U.S. patents (USPTO) and the 1790–1873 patent index; Albany ordinances, 1713–28 (Munsell, Annals of Albany); Anne Grant, Memoirs of an American Lady (1808); 1775 Andrews and Eliot letters (J. L. Bell, Boston 1775); company histories (Kalamazoo Public Library, Hagley, PHMC, newspapers); two sled collector’s books. Dates marked c. or ? are approximate or unconfirmed.

Races, collections and fellow sled sites

Official pages for runner-sled racing, a Flexible Flyer museum, and antique-sled research.

    The official site of the New England Runner Sled Championship. NERSA holds The Fastest Runner Sledder in all the Land at Hogback Mountain in Montville, Maine.

  • Lost Valley Ski Area, Auburn, Maine

    Lost Valley hosts runner sled races each winter, including The Fastest Runner Sledder in the Valley, held with the New England Runner Sled Association. Check their site for this season’s events.

  • Flexible Flyer Sled Museum at the Moorestown Library

    A museum at the Moorestown Library in Moorestown, New Jersey, devoted to Flexible Flyer sleds.

  • Sled Hill

    A noncommercial site devoted to collecting antique snow sleds, focused mainly on Flexible Flyers, with collector galleries and help identifying sleds.
