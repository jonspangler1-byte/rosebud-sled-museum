About the collection

Four hundred sleds, twenty-five makers, and the stories behind them.

The Rosebud Sled Museum is a private collection of about 400 antique American sleds from 25 makers, four of them from Illinois.

Jonathan Spangler, a historian, genealogist and author, restores sleds, races in an annual vintage sled race, and researches the companies that built them.

Featured makers

The companies that made them

Ten of the great American sled makers.

American Acme

Iceplane

The story begins with American Toy and Novelty Works, opened in York, Pennsylvania, in 1917 by German immigrant Casper Oermann to make cider mills, berry presses and boys’ sleds. In 1927 it merged with Acme Wagon Works of Emigsville to become American Acme Co., which a newspaper that year reported had sold more than 150,000 sleds. Its sleds wore speedy names such as Royal Plane, Sky Plane and Monoplane, until the company turned away from sleds in the 1960s.

Flexible Flyer

S.L. Allen & Co. · Philadelphia

Samuel Leeds Allen, a Philadelphia Quaker born in 1841, built S. L. Allen & Co. on farm and garden tools, then looked for a winter product to keep his skilled workers busy all year. His answer was a steerable sled with flexible runners, patented in 1889 and named the Flexible Flyer. Success came slowly, but by 1939 the company was celebrating its five-millionth sled. Allen died in 1918; his company was divided and sold in 1968.

Paris

Paris Mfg. Co. · South Paris, Maine

Henry Franklin Morton began making sleds in 1861 in a West Sumner, Maine, kitchen, a family business shared with his wife, Lucilla Forbes. The firm moved to Paris Hill and then to South Paris for the railroad, and by the early 1900s it employed hundreds, making everything from painted children’s sleds to toboggans. In September 1909 a Norway, Maine, newspaper reported that Paris had shipped a large consignment of sled materials for Lt. Peary’s North Pole expedition.

Wagner

Wagner Mfg. Co. · Cedar Falls, Iowa

Adam Wagner learned the trade as a hardware clerk in Cedar Falls, opened a one-room shop of his own in 1902, and incorporated it in 1904 as the Wagner Manufacturing Company. Beside barn-door track and door strips it built ornate steel sleds like the Winner, with steel hand sleds patented June 28, 1904, and Daisy coaster wagons. The latest sled advertisements found date from 1923; by then the firm was turning to elevator door hardware, and later garage doors.

Kalamazoo

Kalamazoo Sled Co. · Kalamazoo, Michigan

The Kalamazoo Sled Company grew out of the Page Manufacturing Company, a maker of wooden buggy parts, and was reorganized under its new name on February 14, 1894. By 1905 it had absorbed the Columbia Sled Company, employed more than 100 people, and was counted the largest maker of children’s sleds in the world, best known for its Champion line. The Gladding Corporation bought the company in 1968 and moved operations to Maine in 1972.

Pictured: Speed Demon, c. 1938.

Pratt

Pratt Mfg. Co. · Coldwater, Michigan

Pratt Manufacturing Company of Coldwater, Michigan, issued a sled catalog that the University of Michigan library dates, tentatively, to about 1909. In 1945 Max A. Holbrook of Coldwater filed a patent for a simpler sled steering mechanism, assigned to Pratt and granted in 1951. Its Red Rocket sleds carry the stamp “A Product of Pratt Mfg. Co., Coldwater, Michigan.”

Pictured: “Chieftain” branded Red Rocket, c. 1940–1952.

Sherwood

Sherwood Bros.

Sherwood Bros. Mfg. Co. was founded in Canastota, New York, in 1914 by John and William Sherwood, in the former Patten & Stafford wheel-rake plant on James Street. Its 1920 catalog called the firm “Manufacturers of Sleds and Coaster-Wagons,” with models such as the Speeder and the Auto Bob. In the early 1920s it was one of the companies held by Canastota’s Watson Products Corp.

Hunt, Helm & Ferris

Harvard, Illinois

The company grew from a hay carrier Henry L. Ferris patented in 1883 and first built in the basement of a Harvard hardware store, with partners Charles E. Hunt and Nathan B. Helm. Its five-pointed star marked a long line of farm equipment, and its CannonBall line made sleds, coaster wagons and roller skates, branded so boldly “that the paint still reads across a room.” In 1931 the firm took the name Starline.

Period advertisement, Cannon Ball coaster wagons. Hardware Age, Oct. 8, 1925. Internet Archive, public domain.

09

Standard Novelty Works

William Wills and P. F. Duncan established the Standard Novelty Works in Duncannon, Pennsylvania, in 1904, and its Lightning Guider sleds were made there until 1988. In 1920 the plant was credited with producing more children’s sleds than any other American factory, 1,600 to 1,800 a day. It also built children’s wagons, porch swings and furniture before closing in October 1990.

Period advertisement, Lightning Guider Bob Sled. Hardware Age, Jan. 20, 1917. Internet Archive, public domain.

10

Auto Coaster Wheel Co.

The firm began as the Buffalo Sled Co., in business by 1899 making sleds and shovels. In 1909 it moved into a three-story factory in North Tonawanda, New York, added coaster wagons in 1912, and in July 1920 filed to change its name to the Auto-Wheel Coaster Company. It filed for bankruptcy in 1964, and the old plant was destroyed by fire in 1972.

Period advertisement, Auto-Wheel Coaster Co. Toys and Novelties, Aug. 1920. Internet Archive, public domain.
